## Terminology

### Terminology Status

**Established product-management term; adopted here with an explicit problem-selection definition.**

Product strategy has several schools and operating models. This playbook adopts Marty Cagan/SVPG’s distinction: vision describes the future, product strategy selects the most important problems to solve, discovery determines effective solutions, and delivery builds them.[source](source-index.md#src-cagan-vision-strategy) [source](source-index.md#src-cagan-strategy)

This level replaces the vaguer label **Product Direction**. Any legitimate “direction” work should be expressible as a product-strategy choice, an experience-strategy choice, or a portfolio priority. If it cannot, the extra label adds ambiguity rather than strategic value.

## Definition

Product Strategy is a focused, insight-backed set of choices about **which customer or user problems—and which enabling product problems necessary to address them—a product organization will invest in solving within a defined horizon**, why those problems matter now, what advantage or capability makes the bets credible, and what the organization will deliberately not pursue. Market conditions and business objectives shape the selection; they do not substitute for a problem people actually experience.

In an experience-first culture, Product Strategy must remain traceable to human purposes, experience evidence, and intended human outcomes. It also has to integrate company objectives, market reality, technology and data possibilities, commercial viability, organizational capability, risk, and focus. It chooses problem investments; it does not predetermine every solution.

## Governing question

> **Given the human future we seek, company needs, market and technology insights, and our capabilities and constraints, which few important problems should receive product investment now—and which plausible problems will we not pursue in this horizon?**

## Strategic job

This level allocates scarce product attention. It:

- converts broad vision and evidence into a small number of consequential problem bets;
- connects customer value, user value, company value, and enabling possibilities;
- creates focus by declining or deferring attractive alternatives;
- gives empowered teams strategic context without prescribing their solution;
- identifies where product capabilities could create differentiated or defensible value;
- makes product risk, sequencing, and dependencies visible; and
- prevents stakeholder requests and feature volume from becoming the de facto strategy.

## Unit of analysis and scope

The primary unit is **one strategic problem bet within a declared product or portfolio scope and planning horizon**. A complete Product Strategy is a deliberately small, coherent set of those bets plus their rationale and non-choices.

Declare:

- the product, platform, service, or portfolio boundary;
- the customer, user, buyer, operator, or ecosystem actors affected;
- the business and experience outcomes the strategy serves;
- the horizon within which the problem selection is active;
- the teams or capability domains involved; and
- excluded markets, actors, problems, or solution spaces.

The appropriate horizon varies. Nearer-term product strategy may select current critical problems, while product or company vision reaches further. Do not turn the horizon into a release schedule or present a single project requirement as the whole product strategy.

## Expert and source anchors

Cagan describes product strategy as answering which problems empowered teams should solve. His framework emphasizes focus, insights, action, and active management rather than a list of projects and features.[source](source-index.md#src-cagan-strategy) SVPG’s vision-versus-strategy distinction states that product vision describes where the organization wants to go, product strategy helps decide which problems to solve to get there, discovery finds the tactics, and delivery builds the solution.[source](source-index.md#src-cagan-vision-strategy)

SVPG further argues that strong product strategy begins with critical areas for business success and uses quantitative, qualitative, technology, industry, and market insights to select the few important problems worth pursuing.[source](source-index.md#src-cagan-problem-selection)

This playbook adds an experience-first control: the “customer problem” may not be inferred only from the product or buyer request. It must remain connected to the underlying Human Purpose, Current Experience, opportunities, and Strategic UX Outcome.

## Reasoning formula

```text
PRODUCT STRATEGY
= declared product or portfolio scope and horizon
+ Experience Vision and relevant product/company vision
+ Strategic UX Outcome and company objectives
+ customer, user, market, data, technology, and industry insights
+ organizational capabilities, constraints, risks, and possible advantage
+ a deliberately small set of strategic problem bets
+ rationale for why each problem matters now
+ explicit non-bets, sequencing logic, and dependencies
+ human and business success measures
+ strategic context and autonomy for responsible teams
− feature roadmap, stakeholder wish list, or predetermined solution
```

Each problem bet should be expressible as:

```text
PROBLEM BET
= affected actor and consequential context
+ evidenced unmet condition or obstacle
+ human and organizational value at stake
+ insight or leverage that makes the bet timely and credible
+ success evidence
+ scope and non-choice
− prescribed feature
```

Scores and matrices may support comparison, but they do not create strategy. The decision, rationale, trade-off, and concentration of resources are the strategic work.

## What belongs

- Product or portfolio scope and planning horizon.
- A small number of important problem bets.
- Relevant customer, user, buyer, operator, and ecosystem evidence.
- Company objectives and critical business conditions.
- Quantitative, qualitative, market, competitor, regulatory, data, and technology insights where relevant.
- Existing or developable product and organizational capabilities.
- Strategic leverage, differentiation, or defensibility hypotheses.
- Risks, dependencies, sequencing logic, and constraints.
- Explicit non-bets, deferments, and boundaries.
- Human and business success measures without claiming future impact as achieved.
- Team ownership and enough strategic context to enable discovery.

## What does not belong

- A list of features, projects, epics, releases, or stakeholder requests.
- “Build X” phrased as the strategic problem.
- The Experience Vision or company mission restated as strategy.
- Generic goals such as “grow revenue” or “improve engagement” without a problem-selection logic.
- Every identified opportunity treated as equally important.
- A product roadmap used as proof that the underlying problem choices were deliberate.
- The detailed solution tactic; that belongs to discovery and Levels 10–13.
- Experience qualities and cross-touchpoint policies without product-investment choices; those belong primarily at Level 5.
- An implementation plan, delivery estimate, or resource calendar.
- “Product Direction” as a second label for the same choices.

## Adjacent-level boundaries

**Above — Level 5, Experience Strategy:** Experience Strategy defines the coherent stance for how the experience must evolve and may cross product, service, operational, and policy boundaries. Product Strategy decides which important problems receive product investment under company, market, technology, capability, and viability conditions.

**Below — Level 7, Experience Roadmap and Priorities:** Product Strategy selects problem bets. Level 7 translates the combined Experience Strategy and Product Strategy into actor-specific experience areas and time horizons. A problem bet may affect several experiences; one experience area may require several product and non-product contributors.

**Reciprocal relationship:** Levels 5 and 6 are interdependent, not a one-way cascade. Experience evidence can reshape product bets. Product realities and company strategy can constrain or redirect experience choices. The Experience Vision and Strategic UX Outcome keep the exchange anchored in human value.

## Required evidence

A credible Product Strategy requires:

- a declared product or portfolio scope and decision horizon;
- relevant Human Purpose, Current Experience, and opportunity evidence;
- an Experience Vision and intended human outcome, or explicit acknowledgment that they are provisional;
- explicit company objectives, mandates, threats, or constraints;
- sufficient customer, market, business, technology, data, and capability insight for the decision being made;
- evidence that alternatives were compared, declined, sequenced, or deferred;
- a rationale for why the selected problems matter now;
- proposed human and business success evidence; and
- ownership or decision records showing who set, influenced, approved, or executed the strategy.

Not every project designer will possess the full company-level evidence. In that case, claim only the bounded product-strategy decision the record supports and name the missing context.

## Permitted reconstruction

**Permitted:**

- reconstruct a bounded problem bet when requirements, prioritization records, roadmap themes, research, rejected alternatives, and allocation decisions converge;
- clarify the human problem beneath a documented product initiative without pretending that the clearer language was used historically;
- infer that one area received investment when resourcing and delivery evidence prove it, while leaving the original strategic rationale Unknown if it is not documented;
- use confirmed recollection to clarify an evidenced selection or non-choice; and
- use external research to establish domain, market, competitor, regulation, or technology context as C evidence.

**Not permitted:**

- derive a complete Product Strategy from the shipped feature list;
- claim that an unbuilt item was a deliberate non-bet without evidence of a decision;
- invent market, revenue, competitive, technology, or capability reasoning;
- turn a client requirement or executive order into a designer-owned strategic choice;
- imply that the product team evaluated alternatives when no record supports it;
- treat an external trend as the team’s historical insight; or
- claim product-strategy success from release alone.

When the problem investment is documented but its rationale is not, preserve that split: **the decision may be D while the explanatory strategy remains I or Unknown**.

## Claim, lifecycle, and ownership controls

- **Component availability:** A project can have an Evidenced or Partially evidenced problem bet without evidence of the wider Product Strategy. Do not force one project into a company-wide narrative.
- **Provenance:** Classify problem selection, rationale, non-choices, and success evidence separately. External market context is C unless the historical record demonstrates its use.
- **Lifecycle:** Product Strategy may be **proposed, approved, active, revised, or superseded**. A problem bet may be Targeted; its intervention may be Designed, Validated, Implemented, or Released; its results may be observed or measured. “Released strategy” and “the bet succeeded because it shipped” are unsafe formulations.
- **Ownership:** Product strategy is commonly shared across product, design, engineering, data, and business leadership. Use “I influenced the bet through…,” “I contributed the experience evidence…,” “we jointly selected…,” or “product leadership decided…” according to the record. Strategic influence is valuable; do not inflate it into final decision authority.

## Writing grammar

**Strategy statement formula**

> **To advance [Experience Vision / intended human outcome] and [company objective], we will concentrate product investment on [important problem for actor in context], because [evidence and strategic insight], leveraging [capability or advantage], while not pursuing [non-bet] during [horizon]. We will judge progress through [human and business evidence].**

**Illustrative application**

> To advance low-effort, trusted discovery and support durable listening value, concentrate product investment on the problem of translating ambiguous, moment-based listener intent into steerable discovery. The bet would use contextual and preference signals while preserving explicit user correction. Broad social-listening expansion and creator tooling would remain outside this illustrative horizon; progress would require both reduced discovery effort and evidence of sustained listening value.

This is a hypothetical formulation for the playbook. It is not evidence of Spotify’s actual Product Strategy.

## Canonical output and traceability

The canonical output is a **Product Strategy Decision Record** containing:

- product or portfolio scope, horizon, and decision owners;
- relevant Experience Vision, product/company vision, human outcome, and company objective;
- selected strategic problem bets;
- evidence and insight behind each bet;
- capability or advantage hypothesis;
- risks, dependencies, constraints, and sequencing;
- explicit non-bets and decision rationale;
- human and business success evidence;
- team allocation or strategic context where known;
- provenance, maturity, and ownership; and
- review conditions that could change the strategy.

It must trace upstream to Levels 0–5 without pretending that the hierarchy is linear. It combines with Experience Strategy to shape Level 7. Level 10 solution concepts must trace to a selected problem bet, but no Product Strategy entry should require one predetermined concept to remain true.

## Quality audit

**Pass when:**

- the strategy selects a few consequential problems rather than many outputs;
- human and company value are both visible without one replacing the other;
- insight, leverage, and timing explain why the bets are credible now;
- non-bets demonstrate genuine focus;
- teams retain space to discover solutions;
- the claim’s scope and decision ownership match the evidence; and
- success measures distinguish release from human and business results.

**Fail when:**

- the artifact is a roadmap or feature list with a strategic title;
- the problem statement embeds the preferred solution;
- everything is a priority;
- company goals, technology trends, or customer requests replace strategic reasoning;
- “Product Direction” duplicates rather than clarifies the same decisions;
- design contribution is overstated as sole product-strategy ownership; or
- shipping is treated as proof of value or impact.

**Experience-first leadership signal:** The designer brings deep human and experience evidence into product-investment choices, makes the consequences of focus visible, and protects teams’ freedom to discover solutions while remaining accountable to human and organizational value.
