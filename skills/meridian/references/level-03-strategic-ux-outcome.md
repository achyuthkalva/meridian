## Terminology

### Terminology Status

**Source-adapted label.**

Center Centre uses “UX Outcome” for an improvement in a real person’s life and treats it as a measurable destination for an experience vision. This playbook adds “Strategic” to distinguish this level from local usability targets, feature outcomes, and business results.[source](source-index.md#src-spool-outcomes) [source](source-index.md#src-spool-outcome-metrics)

## Definition

A Strategic UX Outcome is the specific, meaningful, and observable improvement in a defined person’s lived experience that the initiative intends to produce.

It describes the change in the person—not the thing the organization ships. It is strategic when it is consequential enough to guide multiple downstream experience and product decisions while remaining concrete enough to evaluate.

## Governing question

> **Whose lived experience should improve, in what meaningful and observable way, if this work succeeds?**

## Strategic job

This level establishes the human-centered definition of success. It:

- converts opportunity evidence into an explicit intended change;
- gives the Experience Vision a measurable human destination;
- aligns design, product, engineering, operations, and leadership around human value;
- separates output, adoption, business performance, and lived improvement;
- provides the anchor for experience measures and live learning; and
- makes strategic trade-offs testable against the person’s outcome.

## Unit of analysis and scope

The unit is **one material change in the experience of one specific actor or behavioral group within a defined context**.

A useful outcome is:

- larger than completing one interface step;
- smaller and more observable than a broad Experience Vision;
- independent of a particular solution;
- meaningful to the person even if the organization’s business metric is omitted; and
- stable enough to guide several releases or coordinated interventions where appropriate.

If different actors experience different forms of value, write separate outcomes and make relationships or tensions explicit.

## Expert and source anchors

Center Centre defines UX outcomes around identifying whose life will improve and how, positions them as the basis for an experience vision, and distinguishes life improvement from conventional product-use metrics.[source](source-index.md#src-spool-outcomes) [source](source-index.md#src-spool-outcome-metrics)

Spool’s broader strategic-UX argument reinforces outcomes over outputs, experiences over products, and research that builds expertise about current experiences rather than merely validating predetermined functionality.[source](source-index.md#src-spool-strategic)

Theory-of-change and contribution-analysis practice inform this playbook’s separation of intended change, indicators, assumptions, contribution, and causal attribution.[source](source-index.md#src-toc-toolkit) [source](source-index.md#src-magenta-contribution)

## Reasoning formula

```text
STRATEGIC UX OUTCOME
= specific actor or behavioral group
+ relevant context
+ evidenced current condition
+ meaningful better condition
+ observable evidence that the change occurred
+ relationship to the Level 0 human purpose
+ assumptions and boundary conditions
− feature, interface, technology, or delivery output
− business metric used as a substitute for human improvement
```

Keep four objects distinct:

| Object | Meaning |
| :---| :---|
| **Outcome** | The meaningful change in the person’s experience. |
| **Indicator** | Evidence that helps determine whether the change is occurring. |
| **Target** | The desired level or threshold for an indicator. |
| **Business result** | Organizational value that may follow from or coexist with the human change. |

## What belongs

- A specific actor or behavioral group.
- The relevant context and human purpose.
- A clear before-to-after change in lived experience.
- Observable success conditions or experience indicators.
- Baseline conditions where known.
- Human value such as reduced uncertainty, increased agency, safer judgment, easier coordination, or successful progress.
- Inclusion and boundary conditions.
- Assumptions connecting the outcome to organizational value.
- A time horizon where it materially clarifies the claim.

## What does not belong

- “Launch the portal,” “add AI,” or another output.
- Feature adoption, clicks, sessions, or task completion used alone as proof of life improvement.
- Revenue, retention, cost reduction, or compliance used as the outcome itself.
- A future-state narrative; that belongs at Level 4.
- A list of opportunity nodes; that belongs at Level 2.
- “Simple,” “seamless,” “delightful,” or “efficient” without observable meaning.
- A target presented as an achieved result.
- Causal language unsupported by the evaluation design.

## Adjacent-level boundaries

**Above — Level 2, Problem and Opportunity Space:** Level 2 shows the range of evidenced conditions that could improve. Level 3 makes the strategic choice about which human change will define success.

**Below — Level 4, Experience Vision:** Level 3 defines the measurable destination. Level 4 makes that destination tangible as a coherent future lived experience without prematurely prescribing a feature.

## Required evidence

A credible Strategic UX Outcome requires:

- a traceable Level 0 actor, purpose, and context;
- evidence from the Level 1 current experience;
- relevant Level 2 opportunity nodes;
- an explicit strategic choice about the intended human change;
- observable indicators or a plan to establish them;
- a known or explicitly unknown baseline; and
- documented assumptions connecting human and organizational value.

The outcome is not mechanically “discovered” in research. Research constrains and informs it; the team chooses it as a strategic commitment.

## Permitted reconstruction

**Permitted:**

- reconstruct the likely intended human change from documented goals, requirements, design decisions, validation evidence, and released behavior when several sources converge;
- distinguish a later, clarified outcome statement from the historical wording;
- propose indicators for future use when no historical metric existed; and
- use external evidence to explain why the human change matters in the domain.

**Not permitted:**

- claim that the team explicitly used a UX Outcome when it did not;
- turn a shipped capability into proof of achieved human improvement;
- relabel a business KPI as a UX outcome;
- invent a baseline, target, measurement, or user response; or
- claim causal impact from temporal sequence alone.

## Claim, lifecycle, and ownership controls

- **Component availability:** Required for an experience-first strategic narrative. Unknown is safer than a polished but unsupported outcome.
- **Provenance:** A later outcome formulation may be R even when its supporting current-state evidence is D.
- **Lifecycle:** Use **Hypothesized** or **Targeted** for the intended outcome. Designed, Validated, Implemented, and Released describe the intervention or experience intended to produce it. Qualitatively observed, Quantitatively measured, Contribution supported, and Causally attributed describe the strength of outcome evidence. Never say that an outcome itself was “released.” The default for an unmeasured intended outcome is **Targeted**, not achieved.
- **Ownership:** Use “I established,” “I helped frame,” “we aligned on,” or “I reconstructed from the evidence” according to the record.

## Writing grammar

**Formula**

> **For [specific actor] in [context], success means moving from [evidenced current condition] to [meaningful better condition], evidenced by [observable experience indicators], so they can [Level 0 purpose].**

**Illustrative application**

> For listeners who know the kind of moment they want but not the exact music, success means moving from repeated browsing and uncertain sampling to satisfying listening with substantially less search effort and uncertainty, so they can return their attention to the activity music is meant to support.

**Claim-safe variants**

- **Targeted:** “We aimed for listeners to reach satisfying audio with less effort and uncertainty.”
- **Validated:** “Testing indicated that participants could reach an acceptable listening direction with fewer corrections.”
- **Measured:** “Post-release evidence showed [specific observed change] for [defined group and period].”

Do not use the validated or measured form unless the evidence exists.

## Canonical output and traceability

The canonical output is a **Strategic UX Outcome Contract** containing:

- outcome ID and statement;
- actor, context, and Level 0 purpose;
- current condition and supporting Level 1 evidence;
- Level 2 opportunity links;
- intended better condition;
- success, progress, and problem-value indicators where useful;
- baseline, target, time horizon, and data source—or explicit unknowns;
- assumptions, risks, inclusion conditions, and business relationship;
- provenance, lifecycle state, ownership, and publication wording; and
- downstream links to Experience Vision and Experience Strategy.

## Quality audit

**Pass when:**

- the statement describes a meaningful change in a person’s experience;
- the actor, context, before, after, and evidence of change are intelligible;
- the outcome remains valid across several possible solutions;
- indicators measure the human change rather than output alone; and
- lifecycle, ownership, uncertainty, and business linkage are explicit.

**Fail when:**

- a feature, release, adoption metric, or business result substitutes for human improvement;
- the statement is too vague to observe;
- the outcome was reverse-engineered only from the solution;
- a target is written as achieved impact; or
- causality exceeds the evaluation evidence.

**Experience-first leadership signal:** The designer defines success in terms of a person’s improved life, connects it responsibly to organizational value, and keeps delivery accountable to that human change.
