# Worked Example — Follow a Hypothetical Music Experience Through Meridian

This teaching example follows a hypothetical music-discovery experience from human purpose to live learning. It makes the level boundaries concrete without claiming Spotify's internal strategy, priorities, decisions, or outcomes.

## Important case-study note

The following is an **illustrative strategic-design exercise**, not a claim about Spotify’s actual internal strategy, roadmap, research findings, organizational hierarchy, or decision process.

It uses publicly documented Spotify capabilities to make the example realistic. Spotify publicly describes personalization across Home and Search and features such as Discover Weekly, Blend, and DJ.[source](source-index.md#src-spotify-design) Spotify Mixes are personalized around artists, moods, genres, and decades.[source](source-index.md#src-spotify-mixes) In 2026 Spotify also described DJ interactions that can accept text or voice requests such as workout-oriented music.[source](source-index.md#src-spotify-discovery)

The strategic hierarchy below is constructed for teaching purposes.

---

## Level 0 — Human Purpose in Context

### Human territory

People do not fundamentally wake up wanting to “use a music-streaming feature.”

They use music and audio to participate in life.

Representative purposes:

- regulate energy
- focus
- relax
- change mood
- intensify a moment
- discover identity and taste
- remember people or periods of life
- create atmosphere
- connect socially
- avoid silence or boredom

### Chosen territory for the worked example

> **Use music to shape the current moment.**

This territory deliberately sits above Spotify.

---

## Level 1 — Current Experience

### Hypothetical research pattern

People frequently know **what they need music to do** before they know **what they want to hear**.

Examples:

- “I need energy for a workout.”
- “I need something calm enough to work.”
- “I want music for cooking with friends.”
- “I want something familiar but not repetitive.”

Current behavior may involve:

1. opening a streaming service,
2. scanning recent items,
3. checking recommendations,
4. searching vague terms,
5. sampling tracks,
6. skipping several songs,
7. switching playlists,
8. eventually settling.

### Experience tension

The person’s intent is often **contextual and fuzzy**, while traditional music navigation frequently expects **content knowledge**: song, artist, album, genre, or playlist.

---

## Level 2 — Problem and Opportunity Space

### Opportunity statement

> People with clear situational intent but vague content intent can face unnecessary decision burden before reaching satisfying audio.

### Opportunity branches

- translating a moment into musical intent
- reducing browsing burden
- improving trust in recommendations
- allowing lightweight correction
- adapting as the moment changes
- balancing familiarity and novelty
- preserving control when the system predicts incorrectly

### What is intentionally absent

No “AI DJ feature” has yet been selected.

The problem remains solution-neutral.

---

## Level 3 — Strategic UX Outcome

### UX Outcome

> **People who know the kind of moment they want, but not the exact music, can begin satisfying listening quickly and confidently with minimal search effort.**

### Possible experience indicators

These are illustrative measures, not Spotify metrics:

- time from opening the product to sustained listening
- number of exploratory actions before sustained listening
- rate of immediate abandonment from recommendation sessions
- number of corrections needed before satisfaction
- self-reported confidence that recommendations match intent
- successful continuation of the session after initial selection

### Business relationship

A good UX outcome may support engagement or retention, but the human outcome remains distinct from the business result.

---

## Level 4 — Experience Vision

### Illustrative experience vision

> **Music should meet people where they are. A person can express or reveal the kind of moment they are in, and their listening environment responds with music that feels appropriate, personal, and easy to steer — without requiring them to translate life into catalogue navigation.**

### Why this is a vision rather than a feature

It does not require:

- a chat interface,
- an AI DJ,
- a particular recommendation algorithm,
- a playlist,
- a Home-screen widget,
- voice input.

Many future product forms could deliver this experience.

---

## Level 5 — Experience Strategy

### Diagnosis

The listening catalogue is enormous, while a person’s moment is often ambiguous. More choice can increase decision burden precisely when the person wants music quickly.

### Guiding Policy

> **Shift vague-intent discovery from browse-first selection toward intent-aware, low-effort guidance while preserving user agency.**

### Coherent experience actions

1. Recognize situational intent earlier.
2. Ask for less explicit information when the system can infer enough.
3. Make correction easier than restarting.
4. Use prior taste as context, not as a cage.
5. Allow the listening session to evolve with lightweight steering.
6. Keep the person aware that they can override the system.

---

## Level 6 — Product Strategy

### Illustrative strategic problem choice

Assume several problems compete for investment:

- creator monetization tools
- podcast organization
- social listening
- music discovery
- library management
- cross-device continuity

An experience-first product strategy might choose:

> **Prioritize the “vague intent → satisfying listening” problem because it is frequent, central to Spotify’s core listening value, strengthens personalization advantage, and can improve the path from opening the product to receiving value.**

### Explicit non-choice

For this planning horizon, do not invest equally in every discovery problem. Concentrate on situations where the person knows **the context or desired feeling** but does not know **the content**.

This is product strategy as focus, not a feature list.

---

## Level 7 — Experience Roadmap and Priorities

### Illustrative experience portfolio

The selected product-strategy bet still contains several actor-specific experience areas that could compete for attention:

- arrive at music that suits an immediate context when content intent is vague;
- recover when an initial recommendation misses the intended feeling;
- steer a listening session without abandoning or rebuilding it;
- continue an intended listening state across devices or changing contexts; and
- reconcile different tastes in a shared listening situation.

### Illustrative prioritization

| Horizon | Experience area | Rationale |
| :---| :---| :---|
| **Now** | Move from vague situational intent to satisfying listening | Most directly serves the selected problem bet and Level 3 outcome. |
| **Next** | Correct and steer a session without restarting | Protects agency and trust once the initial path works. |
| **Later / contingent** | Preserve intent across contexts and shared settings | Potentially valuable, but not required to prove the immediate strategy. |

Library organization, creator tooling, and broader social-listening experiences may remain legitimate product opportunities, but they are outside this illustrative experience portfolio for the planning horizon.

### Decision test

Any proposed roadmap item can now be tested:

> Which prioritized actor experience does this improve, and why should that experience receive attention in this horizon?

This level sequences experience areas. It does not prescribe an AI DJ, chat surface, recommendation control, or any other solution mechanism.

---

## Level 8 — Critical Experience

### Critical Experience

> **Find something that fits this moment without knowing what to search for.**

### Experience boundaries

**Starts when:**  
The person decides they want music but does not have a specific item in mind.

**Ends when:**  
They enter a listening session that feels sufficiently appropriate and stable that active searching stops.

### Supporting experiences

- express the moment
- understand the first recommendation
- correct the direction
- increase/decrease novelty
- continue without repeatedly re-specifying intent

### Why this is larger than a feature

The experience might cross:

- Home
- Search
- recommendation services
- playback
- history
- taste model
- session controls
- voice/text input
- playlist generation

Yet the person experiences one coherent outcome.

---

## Level 9 — Scenarios and Journeys

### Scenario

**Person:** Maya, a frequent Spotify listener.  
**Context:** Weekday, 7:10 PM, at the gym after a mentally exhausting workday.  
**Trigger:** She puts on headphones before a 45-minute workout.  
**Intent:** Energetic music that helps her transition from fatigue into movement.  
**Knowledge:** She does not have a particular artist or song in mind.  
**Constraint:** She does not want to spend several minutes browsing or typing.  
**Preference tension:** Familiar enough to feel immediately comfortable, but not the same workout playlist she has heard repeatedly.  
**Desired outcome:** Start appropriate music quickly, then make only lightweight corrections.

### Journey

```text
Need energy
   ↓
Open Spotify
   ↓
Communicate or reveal workout intent
   ↓
Receive a personalized starting direction
   ↓
Hear first tracks
   ↓
Lightly steer if needed
   ↓
Settle into listening
   ↓
Continue workout with minimal attention on the app
```

### Key design requirement

The best experience is not the one with the most interaction. Once the system understands enough, **attention should return to the workout**.

---

## Level 10 — Solution Concepts

Generate several alternatives before selecting one.

### Concept A — Conversational intent

Maya says or types:

> “High-energy workout music, mostly familiar, but give me a few surprises.”

### Concept B — Context chips

A fast entry surface offers:

```text
Workout · Focus · Commute · Relax · Social
```

Then one lightweight refinement:

```text
Familiar ←→ Discover
```

### Concept C — Contextual session start

The system infers likely workout context from historical behavior and offers one-tap confirmation:

> “Workout energy?”

### Concept D — Adaptive station

A personalized session starts from recent listening and exposes only two steering controls:

```text
More energy
More discovery
```

### Concept decision

Suppose testing shows that a **hybrid of conversational intent + lightweight steering** gives the strongest balance of specificity, speed, and control.

This remains hypothetical.

---

## Level 11 — Enabling Capabilities and Features

The selected concept now requires mechanisms.

### User-facing capabilities

- prominent “start by intent” entry point
- text/voice intent input
- personalized session generation
- lightweight steering controls
- ability to restart/reframe intent
- familiar playback controls

### System capabilities

- natural-language intent parsing
- context classification
- taste/profile retrieval
- recent listening/history retrieval
- recommendation orchestration
- playlist/session generation
- feedback signal capture
- state persistence
- explanation metadata where needed

### Existing Spotify parallels

Spotify publicly documents DJ as a personalized guide and, in 2026, described text/voice DJ requests for situations such as workout music.[source](source-index.md#src-spotify-discovery) Spotify also publicly describes personalized Mixes and personalization across Home and Search.[source](source-index.md#src-spotify-mixes) [source](source-index.md#src-spotify-design)

These existing capabilities make the teaching example plausible, but the design below remains hypothetical.

## Level 12 — Interaction Architecture

### Interaction principle

> **State intent once; steer instead of restarting.**

### Primary flow

```text
HOME
  ↓
Start by intent
  ↓
VOICE / TEXT INPUT
  ↓
System interprets request
  ↓
Preview of session direction
  ↓
START SESSION
  ↓
Listening
  ├── More energy
  ├── More familiar
  ├── More discovery
  └── Change direction
```

### System initiative model

| Moment | User responsibility | System responsibility |
| :---| :---| :---|
| Start | Express rough intent | Understand incomplete language |
| Generate | Confirm/accept | Combine intent + taste + context |
| Listen | Listen | Maintain coherent session |
| Correct | Give lightweight signal | Adjust without resetting context |
| Continue | Minimal attention | Adapt using ongoing feedback |

### Error / recovery model

If intent interpretation is uncertain:

- do not force a long questionnaire,
- show the interpreted direction,
- provide one-tap correction,
- preserve the original request,
- allow rephrasing.

## Level 13 — Interface Definition and Prototyping

### Screen 1 — Home entry

Purpose: expose a fast route for vague intent without overwhelming the existing Home hierarchy.

Possible component:

```text
What do you want to hear right now?
[ Ask Spotify… ]  🎙
```

### Screen 2 — Intent capture

```text
Tell me the moment, mood, activity,
or kind of sound you want.

“Energetic workout music.
Mostly familiar, with some surprises.”
```

States:

- idle
- listening
- transcribing
- interpreting
- no speech detected
- ambiguous intent
- unsupported request

### Screen 3 — Direction preview

```text
WORKOUT · HIGH ENERGY
Mostly familiar · Some discovery

Built around your taste

[ Start ]
[ Adjust ]
```

### Screen 4 — Listening state

Primary attention remains on playback.

Secondary steering:

```text
More energy   More discovery
```

Additional state under overflow:

```text
Change direction
Use less of this
Why this track?
```

### Prototype purpose

Test the **experience hypothesis**, not the beauty of the screens:

> Can Maya move from vague situational intent to satisfying listening quickly, confidently, and with little attention cost?

---

## Level 14 — Validation and Implementation Definition

### Validation plan

Recruit listeners who often choose music based on activity/mood rather than known content.

Test scenarios including:

1. workout with vague musical intent,
2. deep work with strong exclusions,
3. social gathering requiring mixed tastes,
4. request the system interprets incorrectly,
5. desire to change the session halfway through.

### Experience-level success criteria

Illustrative only:

- participant can express intent without learning a command syntax,
- first session direction feels understandable,
- correction is easier than restarting,
- interaction demand drops once playback begins,
- participant retains a clear sense of control,
- experience is meaningfully better than browse/search behavior for vague intent.

### Implementation definition

Design and engineering specify together:

**UI**
- component hierarchy
- responsive behavior
- accessibility names
- keyboard/screen-reader interactions
- voice-input affordances

**State**
- input states
- parsing states
- generation states
- session state
- reframe state
- offline/network errors

**Data**
- intent representation
- taste/profile inputs
- session context
- feedback events

**Service contracts**
- request schema
- generation response
- confidence/ambiguity handling
- failure states

**Measurement**
- intent submitted
- session generated
- session started
- correction used
- session abandoned
- sustained listening threshold
- post-session qualitative signal if appropriate

**Privacy / trust**
- what context is used
- what is inferred
- what is stored
- how users can control or correct personalization

## Level 15 — Delivery, Live Experience, and Outcome Learning

### Delivery

The team implements:

- intent-input surface
- speech/text processing integration
- session orchestration
- recommendation integration
- state persistence
- steering actions
- analytics instrumentation
- accessibility behavior
- error recovery
- performance safeguards

### Cross-functional verification

Before rollout:

- engineering QA verifies functional correctness,
- design QA verifies behavior and hierarchy,
- accessibility verification tests assistive use,
- research validates remaining high-risk assumptions,
- product verifies strategic fit and rollout criteria.

### Release

Use an appropriate rollout mechanism:

- limited cohort
- market/language availability
- experiment
- staged percentage rollout

### Live experience and outcome learning

Do not ask only:

> “Did people use the new feature?”

Return to the Level 3 outcome:

> **Are people with vague intent reaching satisfying listening with less effort and uncertainty?**

If usage rises but the experience outcome does not improve, the product has created output without achieving the intended experience change.

That evidence flows back into the hierarchy and can change the outcome, strategies, or experience priorities rather than merely generating another feature request.
