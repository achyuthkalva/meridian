## Terminology

### Terminology Status

**Playbook-synthesized level; source-adapted strategic practice.**

“Experience Roadmap and Priorities” is Meridian's controlled label for strategic UX roadmapping, experience-wide prioritization, and problem-oriented themes.[source](source-index.md#src-nng-roadmaps) [source](source-index.md#src-spool-cross-experience-priorities) [source](source-index.md#src-spool-roadmap-themes)

The roadmap is a living strategy artifact. It expresses actor-centered experience priorities and horizons while preserving the diagnosis, policies, and product bets that justify them.

## Definition

Experience Roadmap and Priorities is the living, evidence-controlled strategy artifact that shows **which actor-specific experience areas an organization has chosen to improve, sequence, defer, or exclude across planning horizons—and why**.

It translates the combined choices of Experience Strategy and Product Strategy into an experience-first focus model before the work is decomposed into Critical Experiences, scenarios, concepts, capabilities, features, and interfaces.

An **experience area** is a meaningful planning theme in a person’s pursuit of a purpose—such as beginning a regulated application with confidence, coordinating a cross-role review, or recovering from a rejected submission. It is broader than one bounded Critical Experience but narrower and more human than an entire product or department.

## Governing question

> **Which actor-specific experience areas deserve coordinated attention now, next, and later—and what evidence, strategic rationale, dependencies, and non-choices justify that sequence?**

## Strategic job

This strategy level makes focus operational without collapsing into features. It:

- turns strategy into a visible portfolio of human experience areas;
- exposes which actors and situations receive attention and which do not;
- coordinates priorities across products, channels, roles, and departments;
- prevents local feature roadmaps from fragmenting the end-to-end experience;
- balances human importance, strategic alignment, organizational value, risk, feasibility, and learning;
- communicates now/next/later intent without pretending that distant work is certain; and
- provides a defensible selection boundary for defining Critical Experiences at Level 8.

## Unit of analysis and scope

There are two nested units:

1. **Experience-area priority:** one actor or coordinated actor set, one meaningful situation or purpose region, an evidenced current condition, an intended improvement, and a prioritization rationale.
2. **Experience portfolio:** the complete set of candidate, selected, deferred, and excluded experience areas within a declared scope and horizon.

Declare:

- the organization, service, product, portfolio, or initiative boundary;
- the actors represented and omitted;
- the Level 5 and Level 6 choices being operationalized;
- the horizon model, such as Now / Next / Later;
- the decision owner and contributors; and
- the review cadence or conditions that could change priorities.

Avoid false universality. An experience portfolio for one citizen portal is not the state agency’s complete experience portfolio. An internal operations portfolio must not silently stand in for the citizen experience.

## Expert and source anchors

Nielsen Norman Group defines a UX roadmap as a high-level, living artifact that aligns, prioritizes, and communicates future UX work and problems to solve. Its common structure combines scope, goals, themes, and Now / Next / Future horizons; its prioritization guidance treats methods as context-dependent decision aids rather than universal truth.[source](source-index.md#src-nng-roadmaps) [source](source-index.md#src-nng-roadmap-prioritization)

Spool shows why prioritization must consider connections across the wider customer experience rather than local team boundaries, and why problem-oriented themes are safer roadmap commitments than predefined features.[source](source-index.md#src-spool-cross-experience-priorities) [source](source-index.md#src-spool-roadmap-themes)

The specific portfolio level, experience-area definition, formula, and Level 8 boundary below are this playbook’s synthesis.

## Reasoning formula

```text
CANDIDATE EXPERIENCE AREA
= specific actor or coordinated actors
+ meaningful situation, purpose region, or lifecycle context
+ evidenced current condition
+ intended experience improvement
+ relationship to Strategic UX Outcome and Experience Vision
+ scope boundary
− feature, screen, channel, or implementation mechanism

PRIORITY RATIONALE
= human consequence and frequency or reach where known
+ Experience Strategy alignment
+ Product Strategy alignment
+ organizational value, urgency, obligation, or risk
+ evidence strength and confidence
+ feasibility, capability, dependency, and sequencing conditions
+ learning value and reversibility
− fabricated score or certainty

EXPERIENCE PORTFOLIO
= selected, deferred, and excluded experience areas
+ Now / Next / Later or equivalent horizons
+ rationale, owners, dependencies, assumptions, and review triggers
+ links to the Critical Experiences that operationalize each selected area
```

There is no universal arithmetic that can produce the correct priority. Scores can structure discussion, but the underlying evidence, judgment, trade-offs, and decision authority must remain visible. Do not fabricate reach, impact, confidence, or effort numbers to make the portfolio appear rigorous.

## What belongs

- Actor-specific experience areas written in human-outcome language.
- Current-experience and opportunity evidence behind each area.
- Connection to Human Purpose, Strategic UX Outcome, and Experience Vision.
- Experience Strategy and Product Strategy alignment.
- Human consequence, inclusion, safety, legal obligation, and ecosystem effects.
- Relevant organizational value, urgency, risk, reach, or frequency.
- Evidence strength and uncertainty.
- Feasibility, capability, policy, data, technology, and dependency considerations.
- Now / Next / Later or another explicit horizon model.
- Candidate, selected, deferred, and excluded areas.
- Cross-team owners, collaborators, and review triggers.
- The Critical Experiences selected within each active area.

## What does not belong

- A list of features, components, epics, tickets, screens, or releases.
- “Build an AI assistant,” “add notifications,” or another solution commitment.
- Product names or departments used as substitutes for experiences.
- Generic journey stages such as “awareness” or “onboarding” without actor, context, evidence, and intended improvement.
- The strategic problem bet itself without translation into affected lived experiences.
- A detailed scenario, journey sequence, or workflow; those belong at Level 9.
- A bounded Critical Experience definition; that belongs at Level 8.
- A roadmap date presented as a contractual release promise.
- Priority based only on the loudest stakeholder, easiest implementation, or current team boundary.
- A weighted score whose inputs are guesses hidden behind decimals.
- An “everything is important” portfolio with no deferments or exclusions.

## Adjacent-level boundaries

**Above — Level 6, Product Strategy:** Product Strategy selects the important problems that deserve product investment. Level 7 identifies the specific actor-centered experience areas through which those bets and the Experience Strategy become actionable across horizons. Product bets can span several experiences; some priority experiences also require policy, operations, support, or service changes beyond the product.

**Below — Level 8, Critical Experience:** Level 7 manages the portfolio and sequencing of broad experience areas. Level 8 takes one selected area and defines the bounded, consequential experience that must work—its actor, trigger, entry and exit, intended result, qualities, failure, and evidence. For example, **moment-based discovery** may be a Level 7 area; **find something fitting without knowing what to search for** is a Level 8 Critical Experience.

**Artifact boundary:** The Experience Roadmap is strategic, but it does not erase the distinct decisions above it. Level 5 owns the experience diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent actions. Level 6 owns product problem bets. Level 7 integrates those choices into actor-centered priorities across horizons.

## Required evidence

A credible Experience Portfolio requires:

- a declared scope, horizon model, owner, and represented actors;
- a sufficiently complete map of relevant actors, experience areas, and consequential contexts;
- Current Experience and opportunity evidence for candidate areas;
- an intended human outcome and Experience Vision;
- explicit Experience Strategy and Product Strategy inputs;
- relevant business, policy, legal, operational, market, technology, and capability constraints;
- evidence or judgment for human importance, urgency, risk, reach, or frequency where claimed;
- comparison of plausible alternatives, including deferments and exclusions;
- dependencies and cross-functional contributors; and
- documented uncertainty and review conditions.

The portfolio does not require perfect quantitative data. It does require visible reasons. When reach, effort, impact, or feasibility is unknown, state that rather than converting intuition into invented precision.

## Permitted reconstruction

**Permitted:**

- reconstruct a historical portfolio view from dated roadmaps, project sequencing, research priorities, resource allocation, requirements, and delivery records;
- identify the actor-centered experience areas implicit across several documented initiatives;
- distinguish what was demonstrably prioritized from the later experience-first language used to describe it;
- record alternative explanations when sequencing may have reflected contractual, regulatory, or staffing constraints rather than experience value;
- use confirmed recollection to clarify a documented priority decision; and
- use external research to explain domain urgency or obligations without asserting the team used that evidence.

**Not permitted:**

- infer a full prioritization rationale from delivery order alone;
- treat every shipped feature as an intentionally prioritized experience;
- claim that an unbuilt area was deliberately deferred rather than simply unnoticed;
- invent prioritization workshops, scores, votes, stakeholder alignment, or roadmap ownership;
- convert a feature backlog into experience language and present it as the original portfolio;
- use present-day priorities to rewrite the historical sequence; or
- claim that a prioritized experience improved because its associated feature shipped.

When sequencing is documented but rationale is missing, state **what happened** separately from **why it may have happened**.

## Claim, lifecycle, and ownership controls

- **Component availability:** A portfolio may be Evidenced, Partially evidenced, Reconstructed, Unknown, or Not applicable. A single priority does not prove the existence of a managed portfolio.
- **Provenance:** Classify the experience-area formulation, priority decision, rationale, horizon, and outcome evidence separately. A delivered initiative may be D while its placement in a retrospective experience portfolio is R.
- **Lifecycle:** An experience-area priority may be described as **candidate, proposed, selected, active, deferred, removed, or revisited** when records support it. The roadmap is living and should carry a date or version. Designed, Implemented, and Released apply to downstream interventions; observed or measured change applies to outcomes. “Completed priority” must not imply that the human experience is permanently solved.
- **Ownership:** Use “I mapped,” “I facilitated prioritization,” “I recommended,” “I influenced,” “we selected,” or “leadership committed” according to the record. Distinguish decision facilitation from final funding or roadmap authority.

## Writing grammar

**Experience-area formula**

> **Improve [specific actor’s] experience of [meaningful situation or purpose region] from [evidenced current condition] toward [intended human condition], because [human and strategic rationale], subject to [important dependency, risk, or uncertainty].**

**Roadmap-entry formula**

> **[Horizon] — [Experience-area statement]. Priority rationale: [evidence + strategy alignment + human/organizational value]. Dependency or uncertainty: [condition].**

**Illustrative application**

```text
NOW — Moment-based discovery
Help listeners move from a vague desired state to fitting audio without a long
search or browse sequence. Prioritized because it directly supports the target
human outcome and exposes a recurring decision burden. Confidence depends on
evidence across different contexts and accessibility needs.

NEXT — Steer and recover
Help listeners understand and cheaply correct a poor direction without restarting.
Sequenced after the team learns how people express intent and interpret system behavior.

LATER — Cross-context continuity
Help listeners carry a useful listening direction across changing activities and devices.
Deferred until continuity value, privacy expectations, and enabling capabilities are clearer.
```

These entries illustrate an experience-first portfolio structure. They are not claims about Spotify’s actual priorities.

## Canonical output and traceability

The canonical output is an **Experience Portfolio and Roadmap** containing:

- portfolio scope, actors, horizon model, date, owner, and contributors;
- candidate, selected, active, deferred, and excluded experience areas;
- actor-centered statements for every area;
- current evidence and intended human improvement;
- links to Experience Strategy, Product Strategy, Strategic UX Outcome, and Experience Vision;
- prioritization rationale and evidence confidence;
- human, organizational, ethical, policy, and risk considerations;
- dependencies, assumptions, and review triggers;
- non-choices and unresolved gaps; and
- Level 8 Critical Experience links for selected areas.

It must trace upstream to Levels 0–6. Each selected area should produce one or more Critical Experience Definitions at Level 8. Features, concepts, and delivery work may be cross-referenced, but must not replace the experience-area formulation.

## Quality audit

**Pass when:**

- priorities are stated as actor-centered experience areas rather than outputs;
- the portfolio shows why some areas are Now, Next, Later, deferred, or excluded;
- human importance and organizational reality are evaluated together;
- cross-experience and cross-team dependencies are visible;
- uncertainty increases appropriately in later horizons;
- each selected area can be decomposed into bounded Critical Experiences; and
- roadmap changes can be explained through new evidence or changed strategy.

**Fail when:**

- renaming features with the word “experience” is the only transformation;
- priorities mirror the organization chart or backlog rather than people’s lives;
- every item is high priority;
- scores conceal assumptions or fabricate precision;
- horizon labels are disguised release commitments;
- the roadmap lists work without a strategic rationale; or
- portfolio ownership, historical rationale, or realized impact is overstated.

**Experience-first leadership signal:** The designer helps leaders allocate attention around consequential human experiences, makes cross-organizational dependencies and exclusions visible, and preserves traceability from strategic intent to the Critical Experiences teams will actually design.
