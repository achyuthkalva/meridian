## Terminology

### Terminology Status

**Established but materially overloaded term; scope-controlled definition.**

“Experience strategy,” “UX strategy,” “customer-experience strategy,” and “design strategy” are used differently across the field. Jared Spool’s 2026 framing places experience strategy at executive level, directly connecting improvements in customers’, users’, and employees’ lives to an organization’s highest priorities.[source](source-index.md#src-spool-experience-strategy) [source](source-index.md#src-spool-experience-strategists) Atlassian practitioners describe a product-level practice that translates business decisions into experience decisions through diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent actions.[source](source-index.md#src-atlassian-strategy) UXReactor's public framework also presents experience strategy as connected organizational practice rather than isolated interface work.[source](source-index.md#src-uxreactor-5d)

This playbook does not pretend those usages are identical. Every application must declare its scope.

## Definition

Experience Strategy is a coherent, evidence-informed set of choices about **how people’s experience must evolve** to move from the current reality toward the Experience Vision while advancing relevant organizational priorities.

At minimum, it contains:

1. a **diagnosis** of the decisive experience challenge;
2. a **guiding policy** that states the chosen stance and trade-offs; and
3. **coherent experience actions**—mutually reinforcing decisions about the experience and the organizational behavior needed to support it.

It is not a promise to make everything “simple” or “delightful.” Strategy concentrates effort, excludes plausible alternatives, and creates consistency across later decisions.

## Governing question

> **Given the current experience, intended human outcome, future vision, and relevant organizational priorities, what coherent experience choices will guide how we move forward—and what will we deliberately not optimize for?**

## Strategic job

This level turns aspiration into a usable choice system. It:

- identifies the central experience challenge rather than treating every problem equally;
- translates human and organizational evidence into an explicit strategic stance;
- defines trade-offs and non-choices before solution concepts dominate;
- aligns decisions across products, channels, roles, policies, and operations;
- gives teams principles with causal reasoning, not decorative adjectives;
- connects improved human value to relevant organizational value; and
- provides Level 6 and Level 7 with experience constraints against which product investment and sequencing can be judged.

## Unit of analysis and scope

The primary unit is **one strategy kernel for a declared experience challenge at a declared organizational scope**.

Every use must choose one of these scope types:

- **Organizational experience strategy:** executive priorities, enterprise-level human outcomes, organizational capability, culture, governance, and cross-organizational orchestration. This is closest to Spool’s emerging experience-strategy framing.
- **Portfolio or service experience strategy:** coherent experience choices spanning several products, channels, departments, or service components.
- **Project or product experience strategy:** choices governing a bounded initiative, product area, or service episode. This is the scope most historical project case studies can credibly support.

A smaller scope is not less strategic when the challenge and choices are consequential. It is less broad. A project-level kernel must not be described as the company’s experience strategy, and one design principle must not be stretched into a complete strategy.

## Expert and source anchors

Spool describes experience strategy as executive-level UX work that integrates critical organizational objectives with significant improvements in people’s lives. His examples emphasize choosing work from top organizational priorities, conducting strategic research, orchestrating across the organization, and guiding teams toward delivery.[source](source-index.md#src-spool-experience-strategy) [source](source-index.md#src-spool-experience-strategists)

Furness and Tapia’s Atlassian practice offers a more directly operational project/product model: translate business decisions into experience decisions, diagnose the current state, define a broad guiding response, and express it through coherent experience actions. Their work adapts Richard Rumelt’s strategy kernel rather than reducing strategy to vision statements or design principles.[source](source-index.md#src-atlassian-strategy)

UXReactor's public framework reinforces the need to connect strategy, research, experience design, and organizational practice rather than treating any one activity as sufficient.[source](source-index.md#src-uxreactor-5d)

The playbook combines these sources only after declaring scope. It does not attribute the project-level kernel definition to Spool’s executive-level branch.

## Reasoning formula

```text
EXPERIENCE STRATEGY
= explicit organizational scope and planning horizon
+ DIAGNOSIS
   = decisive experience challenge
   + current-experience and opportunity evidence
   + relevant organizational priority or constraint
   + explanation of why this challenge is pivotal
+ GUIDING POLICY
   = chosen experience stance
   + governing trade-offs
   + explicit non-choices
+ COHERENT EXPERIENCE ACTIONS
   = mutually reinforcing experience decisions
   + cross-role, channel, policy, and operating implications
   + enabling organizational behavior where relevant
+ link to Strategic UX Outcome and Experience Vision
+ assumptions, risks, and learning conditions
− generic principles, feature list, or roadmap disguised as strategy
```

Diagnosis must do more than repeat every research finding. Guiding policy must narrow the path. Coherent actions must follow logically from the diagnosis and reinforce one another.

## What belongs

- The declared organizational, portfolio, service, product, or project scope.
- The decisive experience challenge and why it is strategically important.
- Relevant human evidence, business context, market context, and operating constraints.
- The chosen stance for overcoming the challenge.
- Trade-offs and explicit non-choices.
- Mutually reinforcing experience decisions.
- Cross-touchpoint, cross-role, cross-channel, policy, or operational implications.
- The relationship between improved human value and relevant organizational priorities.
- Assumptions, risks, ethical tensions, and conditions that could invalidate the strategy.
- Strategic principles only when each is traceable to the diagnosis and changes a real decision.
- A review mechanism for checking whether later work remains coherent with the strategy.

## What does not belong

- The future destination itself; that belongs at Level 4.
- A generic list such as “simple, seamless, delightful, personalized, accessible.”
- A feature roadmap, solution architecture, screen concept, or implementation plan.
- A research summary with no strategic choice.
- A goal or metric without an approach for changing the experience.
- A collection of best practices applicable to almost any product.
- Product-market, technology, or commercial problem bets without the experience stance; those belong primarily at Level 6.
- Every action the team expects to take, regardless of whether the actions reinforce one another.
- An enterprise claim supported only by one project’s design evidence.
- A retrospective narrative whose only logic is “we built X, therefore our strategy must have been Y.”

## Adjacent-level boundaries

**Above — Level 4, Experience Vision:** The vision describes the future experience worth pursuing. Experience Strategy explains the diagnosis and the governing choices for progressing toward it. “People will feel confident and in control” is vision or outcome language; “preserve user agency by making automation steerable and reversible” can be a strategic policy when grounded in evidence and trade-offs.

**Below — Level 6, Product Strategy:** Experience Strategy asks **how the experience must evolve**. Product Strategy asks **which important problems will receive product investment**, considering company needs, market and technology insights, capabilities, and focus. They constrain and inform each other; neither is merely a downstream translation of the other.

**Scope boundary:** At organizational level, Experience Strategy may influence which company priorities become experience initiatives. At project/product level, it governs experience choices inside an already bounded strategic problem. Always state which form is being claimed.

## Required evidence

A credible Experience Strategy requires:

- traceability to the Human Purpose, Current Experience, and relevant opportunity evidence;
- a Strategic UX Outcome and Experience Vision, or explicit acknowledgment that either remains provisional;
- a clearly stated organizational priority, business condition, mandate, or constraint;
- evidence sufficient to identify one decisive experience challenge rather than a symptom list;
- knowledge of relevant actors, channels, policies, operations, and dependencies;
- evidence or rationale for the chosen trade-offs and non-choices;
- cross-functional input from functions materially responsible for the experience; and
- documented assumptions, risks, and signals that would cause the strategy to change.

Market and competitor research may inform the diagnosis, but it is not mandatory for every strategy. Its relevance depends on the challenge. Research quantity does not substitute for strategic choice.

## Permitted reconstruction

**Permitted:**

- reconstruct a project-level strategy kernel from converging evidence of the challenge, repeated decisions, trade-offs, rejected alternatives, requirements, design rationale, and delivery behavior;
- write a later strategic articulation that makes implicit coherence visible, provided it is labeled as reconstructed;
- separate documented decisions from the retrospective diagnosis that best explains their relationship;
- use confirmed recollection to clarify an evidenced trade-off or non-choice; and
- use external research to establish surrounding market, regulatory, technological, or organizational context without claiming that the original team used it.

**Not permitted:**

- claim a formal strategy workshop, executive mandate, or enterprise strategy that did not occur;
- invent the diagnosis simply because it rationalizes the final solution;
- infer a deliberate non-choice merely because a feature was absent;
- treat a set of design attributes as strategy without evidence of trade-offs and coherent action;
- use current external research to manufacture historical causality; or
- describe execution success as proof that the strategy was valid.

When the evidence supports coherence but not contemporaneous articulation, use wording such as **“The evidence supports this retrospective strategy formulation”**, not **“We established this strategy at the outset.”**

## Claim, lifecycle, and ownership controls

- **Component availability:** Experience Strategy may be Evidenced, Partially evidenced, Reconstructed, Unknown, or Not applicable. Unknown is preferable to rebranding ordinary design rationale as strategy.
- **Provenance:** Classify the diagnosis, policy, and each major action separately when their evidence differs. A documented trade-off can be D while the synthesized kernel remains R.
- **Lifecycle:** A strategy may be **proposed, aligned, approved, active, revised, or superseded** when supported by records. It is not “released.” Individual interventions may be Designed, Validated, Implemented, or Released, and outcomes may be observed or measured. Neither release nor outcome movement proves that every strategic choice caused the result.
- **Ownership:** Distinguish setting the strategy, facilitating it, influencing it, translating it into design, or reviewing its application. Use “I set,” “I helped shape,” “I translated,” or “we aligned” according to the record. Do not turn product or executive ownership into sole design ownership.

## Writing grammar

**Kernel formula**

> **Because [diagnosis supported by evidence], we will [guiding policy and decisive trade-off], expressed through [coherent experience actions], while deliberately not [non-choice], to move [actor] from [current condition] toward [Strategic UX Outcome and Experience Vision] and support [relevant organizational priority].**

**Illustrative application**

```text
Scope:
Project/product experience strategy for moment-based audio discovery.

Diagnosis:
When listeners know the state they want but not the content to request,
catalog browsing transfers too much interpretation and decision effort to them.

Guiding policy:
Shift from browsing-first discovery toward intent-sensitive guidance that is
lightweight to steer, understandable enough to trust, and always reversible.

Coherent experience actions:
- recognize the listener’s situation before demanding a precise query;
- reduce choice without removing meaningful control;
- let the listener correct direction with low effort;
- preserve continuity as context changes; and
- treat uncertainty and recovery as first-class parts of discovery.

Non-choice:
Do not maximize passive automation at the cost of agency or understanding.
```

This illustrates the writing structure. It is not a claim about Spotify’s actual strategy.

## Canonical output and traceability

The canonical output is an **Experience Strategy Kernel** containing:

- scope, horizon, owner, and collaborators;
- concise diagnosis with evidence references;
- guiding policy;
- three to seven coherent experience actions;
- trade-offs and explicit non-choices;
- relationship to the Strategic UX Outcome, Experience Vision, and organizational priorities;
- affected actors, channels, policies, operations, and capabilities;
- assumptions, risks, and change conditions;
- provenance and lifecycle state; and
- decision tests for reviewing downstream work.

It must trace upstream to Levels 0–4. It informs Level 6 Product Strategy and Level 7 Experience Roadmap and Priorities in a reciprocal loop. Downstream concepts, capabilities, and interface decisions should be able to cite the specific strategic choice they enact.

## Quality audit

**Pass when:**

- the scope and strategic challenge are explicit;
- the diagnosis explains what is decisive rather than listing everything wrong;
- the guiding policy changes how choices will be made;
- non-choices show genuine focus;
- coherent actions reinforce one another and can guide several solution paths;
- the strategy connects human improvement to relevant organizational value; and
- later decisions can be accepted, rejected, or revised by reference to it.

**Fail when:**

- the “strategy” is vision, aspiration, principles, tactics, or roadmap alone;
- any generic design team could reuse it unchanged;
- the actions do not follow from the diagnosis;
- it attempts to improve everything simultaneously;
- it prescribes the final feature before discovery;
- the claimed scope exceeds the evidence; or
- reconstructed coherence is presented as historical strategic intent.

**Experience-first leadership signal:** The designer converts human evidence and organizational reality into hard, coherent experience choices; makes trade-offs visible; and helps multiple functions act consistently without prematurely dictating the solution.
