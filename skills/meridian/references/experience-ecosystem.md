# Experience Ecosystem — See the Whole System Before Narrowing the Work

This guide helps Meridian recognize the wider system that shapes an experience. Use it when people, organizations, channels, policies, technologies, or dependencies interact across the boundary of one product or team.

> [!NOTE]
> Experience Ecosystem is a cross-level orientation and traceability view. It strengthens the Level 0–15 chain; it does not become a seventeenth level.

## Terminology

### Terminology status

**Established concept; Meridian-controlled application.**

An **experience ecosystem** is the dynamic network of people, organizations, products, services, channels, information, rules, resources, and relationships that can shape an actor's experience. An **experience ecosystem map** is an evidence-controlled representation of that network within a declared boundary.[source](source-index.md#src-nng-ecosystem-map) [source](source-index.md#src-service-ecosystem-design) [source](source-index.md#src-customer-ecosystem-actors)

The distinction matters. The ecosystem continues to change even when the map is not updated. No single organization fully controls a multi-actor ecosystem.

## Use the ecosystem view when the experience crosses boundaries

Use this guide when one or more of these conditions appear:

- several actors have different purposes, risks, or levels of power;
- an experience crosses products, services, channels, departments, or organizations;
- policy, operations, data, technology, or incentives shape what a person can do;
- a platform connects producers, consumers, administrators, partners, or regulators;
- a handoff, dependency, or indirect actor can change the outcome; or
- local improvements may move friction or harm elsewhere in the system.

For a narrow, single-actor task inside a stable product boundary, the normal level chain may be enough. Do not create a decorative map when it will not change understanding or decisions.

## Map the system in five evidence-led moves

### 1. Declare the boundary and focal experience

State the purpose of the map, the focal actor or actor set, the time frame, the system boundary, and what is intentionally excluded. A boundary makes the map usable; it must not be mistaken for the limits of the real ecosystem.

### 2. Inventory actors, purposes, and sources

List consequential human actors separately. Record each actor's purpose, context, constraints, risks, influence, and evidence source. Add organizations only after identifying the people whose actions and decisions give those organizations meaning.

### 3. Connect the elements that shape experience

Map only relationships that matter to the focal experience:

- products, services, objects, information, money, and resources;
- devices, channels, physical settings, and assisted-service paths;
- actions, handoffs, dependencies, feedback, and delays;
- policies, norms, incentives, authority, and power; and
- assumptions, mental models, and conflicting interpretations.

Label direction and meaning. A line without a named relationship hides more than it explains.

### 4. Test the map for systemic effects

Ask where experience is created, constrained, transferred, or lost. Look for feedback loops, gatekeepers, bottlenecks, conflicting incentives, missing actors, invisible labor, unequal power, and changes that help one actor while harming another.

### 5. Keep the map alive through the level chain

Link ecosystem evidence to the Meridian levels it informs. Record unknowns and contradictions. Revisit the map when the boundary, actors, policy, channels, dependencies, or live evidence changes.

## Include enough structure to support a decision

A useful Experience Ecosystem record contains:

- purpose and decision supported;
- focal actor or coordinated actor set;
- declared boundary, exclusions, and time frame;
- actors with distinct purposes, contexts, risks, and power;
- organizations, offerings, channels, objects, information, and resources;
- named relationships, flows, dependencies, policies, norms, and incentives;
- current evidence, source, date, and testimony distance;
- gaps, contradictions, assumptions, and confidence limits;
- systemic patterns and plausible consequences;
- decisions, owners, review triggers, and affected Meridian levels; and
- links to the actor chains and artifacts that carry the finding forward.

The visual map is optional. The evidence and relationship record is not.

## Keep the ecosystem distinct from adjacent Meridian artifacts

| Boundary | Experience Ecosystem contributes | The adjacent artifact still owns |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Level 0 — Human Purpose | Actors, contexts, institutions, and conditions surrounding human purposes. | A precise account of what each person is trying to accomplish. |
| Level 1 — Current Experience | Cross-channel, cross-actor, and organizational conditions shaping today's experience. | The evidenced current experience of a specific actor. |
| Level 7 — Experience Roadmap and Priorities | Dependencies, actor effects, leverage points, and system risks that influence sequencing. | Strategic prioritization of experience areas across horizons. |
| Level 11 — Capabilities and Features | Shared capabilities and non-product contributors required across the system. | Justified mechanisms that enable selected concepts and experiences. |
| Level 12 — Interaction Architecture | Actors, systems, data, handoffs, and constraints that behavior must coordinate. | The detailed behavioral structure before interface expression. |

Do not use the ecosystem map as a substitute for a journey, service blueprint, organizational chart, technical architecture, stakeholder list, or roadmap. It may connect those views, but each answers a different question.

## Control claims so the map does not manufacture certainty

- Separate observed relationships from stakeholder claims, working hypotheses, and desired future connections.
- Preserve the source, date, speaker, and testimony distance for material claims.
- Do not infer a person's purpose from an organization's objective.
- Do not treat proximity on a diagram as evidence of influence.
- Do not claim that one organization controls an emergent ecosystem.
- Mark missing actors, disputed relationships, and unknown consequences visibly.
- Validate important relationships with the affected actors when possible.

## The ecosystem view succeeds when it changes the work responsibly

The view is useful when it helps Meridian do at least one of these things:

- prevent a consequential actor from disappearing;
- reveal a dependency or constraint before a local solution is chosen;
- separate different actor chains while preserving their relationships;
- improve a strategic priority or sequencing decision;
- identify a non-product change needed for the experience to work; or
- expose a systemic risk, trade-off, or learning question.

If it only makes the documentation larger, reduce or remove it.

## End with a traceable system, not a finished picture

The ecosystem map is a living orientation artifact. Its job is to keep the whole system visible while Meridian makes increasingly specific choices—and to show when new evidence should change those choices.
