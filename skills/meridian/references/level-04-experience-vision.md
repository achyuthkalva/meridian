## Terminology

### Terminology Status

**Established practitioner term; operational definition synthesized for this playbook.**

Experience vision is an established practitioner concept for an aspirational future experience that aligns decisions. There is no single industry-standard syntax, horizon, or artifact. Meridian preserves the shared principles while making evidence and boundary rules explicit.[source](source-index.md#src-spool-vision-steps) [source](source-index.md#src-spool-vision-self-fulfilling) [source](source-index.md#src-uxreactor-tekion)

## Definition

An Experience Vision is a research-grounded, memorable account of the future lived experience an organization is trying to make possible for specific people. It makes the Strategic UX Outcome tangible across relevant situations, relationships, channels, and time without committing the team to a particular feature, interface, technology, or release plan.

The vision is a **destination and decision reference**, not a prediction. It expresses what a substantially better experience would be like if the organization succeeded, while leaving room to discover how to create it.

## Governing question

> **If we substantially realize the intended human outcome, what should people’s future lived experience be like—and what must remain true across the situations that matter?**

## Strategic job

This level gives diverse teams a shared human-centered destination. It:

- converts an abstract outcome into a future experience people can picture;
- keeps long-term decisions anchored in people rather than current technology;
- exposes whether a proposal moves toward or away from the intended future;
- gives product, design, engineering, operations, policy, support, and leadership a common reference;
- makes cross-touchpoint and cross-department implications visible; and
- creates durable direction while allowing near-term solutions to change.

## Unit of analysis and scope

The primary unit is **one coherent future-experience story for a declared actor, ecosystem, and strategic horizon**.

The scope must state:

- the actor or coordinated set of actors;
- the human purpose and contexts covered;
- the organizational, product, service, or project boundary;
- the approximate horizon or planning distance; and
- what remains outside the vision.

Spool reports that many effective visions look five or ten years ahead, but the number is not a rule.[source](source-index.md#src-spool-vision-steps) The horizon should be far enough beyond the committed roadmap to avoid incremental feature planning, yet close enough to remain intelligible and strategically useful. A project-level vision must not be presented as the organization’s enterprise vision.

When several ecosystem actors have materially different purposes, show separate but coordinated viewpoints. Do not hide conflict by writing one frictionless story for a generic “user.”

## Expert and source anchors

Spool describes successful experience visions as research-based, focused on users’ experience, shared across the team, and useful for testing whether decisions move toward or away from a distant destination.[source](source-index.md#src-spool-vision-steps) His later vision writing emphasizes a story that contrasts with the current experience, communicates the design outcome rather than the mechanism, and lets people throughout the organization locate their work within the future.[source](source-index.md#src-spool-vision-self-fulfilling) [source](source-index.md#src-spool-vision-story)

UXReactor's public Tekion case study illustrates a design-driven culture that connects intended experience and cross-functional execution. Meridian therefore treats a vision as a visible, testable future experience outcome rather than a prescribed interface or delivery plan.[source](source-index.md#src-uxreactor-tekion)

The scope declaration, evidence controls, formula, and adjacent-level tests below are this playbook’s synthesis.

## Reasoning formula

```text
EXPERIENCE VISION
= declared actor or coordinated ecosystem
+ strategic horizon and relevant future context
+ meaningful human purpose
+ coherent future experience story or sequence
+ realization of the Strategic UX Outcome
+ essential experience qualities and retained human agency
+ continuity across consequential people, channels, and moments
+ contrast with evidenced current experience
+ visible assumptions, tensions, and scope limits
− feature inventory, interface specification, architecture, or release plan
− unsupported prediction presented as certainty
```

The formula is a completeness check. It does not require every vision to be one sentence, nor does it license invented future detail.

## What belongs

- Specific actors and the future situations in which the experience matters.
- The meaningful progress people can make and why it matters.
- A coherent future sequence, episode, storyboard, or narrative.
- The felt and observable qualities of that experience.
- How current friction, uncertainty, fragmentation, delay, risk, or exclusion would be transformed.
- How people retain understanding, agency, dignity, safety, and recovery.
- Connections across consequential touchpoints, roles, channels, or departments.
- The strategic horizon and declared scope.
- The relationship to the Strategic UX Outcome.
- Research anchors, assumptions, open tensions, and affected actors.
- A memorable form that helps decision-makers apply the vision repeatedly.

## What does not belong

- A slogan such as “delight users through AI.”
- A product mission, brand promise, value proposition, or revenue ambition presented as the experience.
- A feature list, product concept, target architecture, screen sequence, or technology forecast.
- A roadmap, prioritized backlog, operating plan, or delivery date.
- Design principles without a future lived experience.
- The diagnosis, guiding policy, or coherent choices that belong at Level 5.
- A metric list without a story of what changes for people.
- An idealized story that erases policy, accessibility, safety, or ecosystem tensions.
- A future narrative derived only from the solution that was eventually built.

## Adjacent-level boundaries

**Above — Level 3, Strategic UX Outcome:** Level 3 states the intended human change and how it could be observed. Level 4 makes that change tangible as a future lived experience. The outcome can usually be stated compactly; the vision needs enough context and narrative coherence for people to imagine and evaluate decisions against it.

**Below — Level 5, Experience Strategy:** Level 4 describes the destination. Level 5 diagnoses the decisive experience challenge and chooses the policy and coherent actions for moving toward it. A vision says **what the future should be like**; a strategy says **what choices will guide progress**.

**External distinction — Product Vision:** Product vision is commonly framed around the future a product or product portfolio seeks to create. Experience Vision deliberately keeps the person’s lived experience primary and may cross product, service, policy, operational, and offline boundaries. The two may support each other, but they are not automatically interchangeable.[source](source-index.md#src-cagan-vision-strategy)

## Required evidence

A credible Experience Vision requires:

- an explicit Level 0 actor, purpose, context, and human stakes;
- a sufficiently evidenced Level 1 current experience;
- relevant Level 2 opportunities, including variation and counterevidence;
- a Level 3 Strategic UX Outcome or an explicitly provisional outcome;
- evidence about latent needs, workarounds, expectations, and consequential contexts;
- awareness of other actors and organizational functions that shape the experience;
- a declared horizon, scope, and set of assumptions; and
- participation or review from people who would materially influence or deliver the experience.

A vision is not mechanically “found” in research. Research constrains the imagination; the future story remains a deliberate, normative synthesis that must be tested for desirability, coherence, inclusion, and strategic usefulness.

## Permitted reconstruction

**Permitted:**

- synthesize a retrospective vision from documented current-state evidence, goals, design decisions, concepts, and delivered behavior when those sources converge;
- write the clearer future-experience story the evidence supports today, explicitly labeling it as reconstructed;
- retain multiple candidate visions when historical evidence does not resolve a strategic choice;
- use authoritative domain research to test plausibility, accessibility, regulatory, market, or technology context; and
- identify which later design decisions are consistent with the reconstructed vision without claiming the vision caused them.

**Not permitted:**

- claim that the team created, aligned on, or used an Experience Vision when no evidence supports that history;
- present a polished retrospective story as a contemporaneous strategic artifact;
- turn the released solution into the only possible future and call it the vision;
- invent user desires, emotional responses, organizational alignment, or cross-functional influence;
- use present-day technology or competitor research to manufacture the team’s historical reasoning; or
- imply that release or stakeholder approval proves the envisioned experience occurred.

## Claim, lifecycle, and ownership controls

- **Component availability:** Record the vision as Evidenced, Partially evidenced, Reconstructed, Unknown, or Not applicable. A project can contain strong future-oriented decisions without evidence of a formal vision.
- **Provenance:** Separate documented historical vision material from later synthesis. A reconstructed narrative is R even when some inputs are D; external plausibility evidence is C unless the project record connects it to the original decision.
- **Lifecycle:** A vision artifact may be described as **drafted, aligned, approved, active, revised, or superseded** when the record supports that governance state. The vision itself is not “implemented” or “released”; downstream interventions are. Observed outcomes may show movement toward the vision, not automatic realization of the whole vision.
- **Ownership:** Use “I created,” “I facilitated,” “I helped shape,” “we aligned on,” or “I reconstructed” according to the evidence. Facilitating a cross-functional vision is legitimate leadership and must not be rewritten as sole authorship.

## Writing grammar

**Short framing formula**

> **In [horizon], when [actor] is [future context], they can [meaningful progress] through an experience that [coherent future condition and essential qualities], so that [Strategic UX Outcome and Human Purpose] become possible across [scope].**

**Preferred narrative opening**

> **Imagine a future in which [actor and situation]…**

Follow it with a concrete episode that contrasts with today’s evidence. Use experiential verbs such as **understands, decides, coordinates, recovers, trusts, continues, adapts, or completes**. Use technology or product nouns only when they are necessary context, not as the protagonist.

**Illustrative application**

> Five years from now, when a listener moves from a demanding workday into exercise, finding audio no longer becomes a second planning task. The listener can express or adjust the moment’s intent in seconds, understand and steer what follows, and begin with confidence while retaining control as energy and circumstances change.

This is an illustrative future experience, not a claim about Spotify’s strategy or roadmap.

## Canonical output and traceability

The canonical output is an **Experience Vision Package** containing:

- title and memorable short statement;
- actor, ecosystem, scope, and horizon;
- future-experience narrative or storyboard;
- contrast with the evidenced current experience;
- intended human outcome and essential experience qualities;
- consequential cross-touchpoint and cross-role continuity;
- tensions, ethical considerations, assumptions, and exclusions;
- source evidence and provenance;
- ownership and alignment record; and
- decision prompts such as “Does this move us toward the vision?”

It must trace upstream to Levels 0–3. Downstream, it must constrain Level 5 strategy choices, inform Level 6 product bets, and provide Level 7 priorities with a destination. A vision-type, concept film, storyboard, or prototype may communicate the vision, but it must be labeled as an illustrative future—not a validated or committed solution.

## Quality audit

**Pass when:**

- people can picture a coherent future experience rather than recite a slogan;
- the story is traceable to current-experience evidence and the intended human outcome;
- it stays centered on people even when technology appears;
- it extends beyond a single feature or release;
- multiple solution paths could still realize it;
- relevant ecosystem actors, tensions, and scope limits remain visible; and
- teams can use it to judge whether decisions move toward or away from the future.

**Fail when:**

- the product, technology, or interface is the protagonist;
- the vision merely restates the outcome or lists principles;
- the story is untethered aspiration or retrospective solution theatre;
- current constraints make it purely incremental, or speculative technology makes it meaningless;
- the vision hides conflicts between actors or promises impossible perfection; or
- approval, implementation, and realized experience are conflated.

**Experience-first leadership signal:** The designer helps the organization see a desirable human future, makes it concrete enough to guide decisions, and keeps the route open to learning rather than locking the future to today’s solution.
